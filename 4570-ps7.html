MATH 4570 · Problem Set 7 (with solutions)

Source: MIT 18.700 Linear Algebra, Fall 2013 (MIT OpenCourseWare, CC BY-NC-SA), Problem Set 7. Problem 4 is Axler (2nd ed) p.159 ex.8, inlined. Inner product is linear in the first slot. Solutions by Claude.

Problems

1. $V$ a complex inner-product space with ORTHOGONAL basis $(f_1,\dots,f_n)$, and $T\in\mathcal{L}(V)$.

(a) Show every $v\in V$ is $v=\sum_i \frac{\langle v,f_i\rangle}{\langle f_i,f_i\rangle}f_i$. (b) A formula for the $(i,j)$ entry $a_{ij}$ of the matrix of $T$ in $(f_1,\dots,f_n)$. (c) A formula for the entry $b_{ij}$ of the matrix of $T^*$.

(d) How do you pass from $A$ to $B$?

$T(1,2,3)=(0,0,0)$ and $T(2,5,7)=(2,5,7)$.

5. (a) Find a real $a$ so that some self-adjoint $T\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^3)$ has $T(1,2,3)=(0,0,0)$ and

$T(2,5,a)=(2,5,a)$. (b) Find an eigenvector of $T$ not in $\mathrm{span}((1,2,3),(2,5,a))$.

Solutions

↑ Problem

Solution 1. Fourier expansion in an orthogonal basis.

(a) Write $v=\sum_j c_j f_j$. Taking the inner product with $f_i$ and using $\langle f_j,f_i\rangle=0$ for $j\neq i$, $\langle v,f_i\rangle=\sum_j c_j\langle f_j,f_i\rangle=c_i\langle f_i,f_i\rangle$, so $c_i=\frac{\langle v,f_i\rangle}{\langle f_i,f_i\rangle}$, giving $v=\sum_i\frac{\langle v,f_i\rangle}{\langle f_i,f_i\rangle}f_i$.

(b) By definition $Tf_j=\sum_i a_{ij}f_i$, so applying (a) to $Tf_j$: $a_{ij}=\frac{\langle Tf_j,f_i\rangle}{\langle f_i,f_i\rangle}$.

(c) Likewise $b_{ij}=\frac{\langle T^*f_j,f_i\rangle}{\langle f_i,f_i\rangle}=\frac{\langle f_j,Tf_i\rangle}{\langle f_i,f_i\rangle}$ (using $\langle T^*x,y\rangle=\langle x,Ty\rangle$).

(d) Since $\langle f_j,Tf_i\rangle=\overline{\langle Tf_i,f_j\rangle}=\overline{a_{ji}}\,\langle f_j,f_j\rangle$ (as $\langle f_j,f_j\rangle$ is real), $b_{ij}=\overline{a_{ji}}\cdot\frac{\langle f_j,f_j\rangle}{\langle f_i,f_i\rangle}$. With $D=\mathrm{diag}(\langle f_i,f_i\rangle)$ this is $B=D^{-1}A^{*}D$, where $A^{*}$ is the conjugate transpose of $A$. (If the basis is orthonormal, $D=I$ and $B=A^{*}$ - the usual conjugate transpose.) ∎

↑ Problem

Solution 2. $I$, $-I$, and every reflection.

A self-adjoint $S$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$ is a symmetric matrix, hence orthogonally diagonalizable with real eigenvalues; $S^2=I$ forces each eigenvalue to satisfy $\lambda^2=1$, i.e. $\lambda\in\{+1,-1\}$. So the self-adjoint square roots of $I$ are exactly the symmetric matrices with eigenvalues in $\{\pm1\}$:

$\bullet$ both $+1$: $S=I$; $\bullet$ both $-1$: $S=-I$;

$\bullet$ one $+1$ and one $-1$: the reflections $S$ with rows $(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi)$ and $(\sin\varphi,-\cos\varphi)$, $\varphi\in[0,2\pi)$. (Each such $S$ is symmetric with $S^2=I$.) Unlike the unique POSITIVE square root, there are infinitely many. ∎

↑ Problem

Solution 3. $q^2+q+2$ if $\mathrm{char}\neq 2$; $q^2$ if $\mathrm{char}=2$.

$S^2=I$ means the minimal polynomial of $S$ divides $x^2-1=(x-1)(x+1)$. Characteristic $\neq 2$: then $x-1,x+1$ are coprime, so $S$ is diagonalizable with eigenvalues in $\{\pm1\}$: $S=I$ (1 matrix), $S=-I$ (1), or $S$ has eigenvalues $+1,-1$ - determined by an ordered pair of distinct eigenlines (the $+1$ and $-1$ lines). There are $q+1$ lines, giving $(q+1)q$ such $S$. Total $2+q(q+1)=q^2+q+2$. Characteristic $2$: then $x^2-1=(x+1)^2$, so $S^2=I\Leftrightarrow(S+I)^2=0$, i.e. $S=I+N$ with $N^2=0$. The number of $N\in M_2(\mathbb{F}_q)$ with $N^2=0$ is $1+(q^2-1)=q^2$ (the zero matrix and $q^2-1$ rank-one nilpotents). (Check by brute force: $q=2\Rightarrow 4$; $q=3\Rightarrow 14$; $q=5\Rightarrow 32$.) ∎

↑ Problem

Solution 4. Impossible - the two vectors are not orthogonal.

If such $T$ existed, $(1,2,3)$ would be an eigenvector with eigenvalue $0$ and $(2,5,7)$ an eigenvector with eigenvalue $1$. For a self-adjoint operator, eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal. But $\langle(1,2,3),(2,5,7)\rangle=2+10+21=33\neq 0$. Contradiction, so no such $T$ exists. ∎

↑ Problem

Solution 5. (a) $a=-4$. (b) $(-23,10,1)$.

(a) As in problem 4, $(1,2,3)$ (eigenvalue $0$) and $(2,5,a)$ (eigenvalue $1$) must be orthogonal: $\langle(1,2,3),(2,5,a)\rangle=2+10+3a=0\Rightarrow a=-4$. (Such a self-adjoint $T$ then exists: diagonalize with these orthogonal eigenvectors.)

(b) A self-adjoint $T$ has an orthogonal eigenbasis, so the third eigenvector is orthogonal to both $(1,2,3)$ and $(2,5,-4)$ - i.e. their cross product $(1,2,3)\times(2,5,-4)=(-23,10,1)$. This lies outside $\mathrm{span}((1,2,3),(2,5,-4))$ and is an eigenvector of $T$ (found without constructing $T$). ∎
