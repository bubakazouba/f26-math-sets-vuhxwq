17. Suppose $D_4$ is the internal direct product of proper subgroups $H$ and $K$. Since $H\cap K=\{e\}$, the products $hk$ are distinct ($hk=h'k'$ gives $h'^{-1}h=k'k^{-1}\in H\cap K$, so $h=h'$, $k=k'$). So $8=|D_4|=|HK|=|H|\,|K|$. Neither subgroup can be $\{e\}$, because then the other would be all of $D_4$, which is not proper. So $\{|H|,|K|\}=\{2,4\}$.

Every group of order 2 or 4 is abelian: order 2 is cyclic; order 4 is either cyclic or has $x^2=e$ for all $x$, and then $xy=(xy)^{-1}=y^{-1}x^{-1}=yx$. So $H$ and $K$ are abelian.

Now any $g=hk$, $g'=h'k'$ in $D_4$ commute: $gg'=hkh'k'=(hh')(kk')$ and $g'g=h'k'hk=(h'h)(k'k)=(hh')(kk')$, using that elements of $H$ commute with elements of $K$ and that $H,K$ are abelian. So $D_4$ would be abelian. But $srs=r^{-1}\neq r$ (as $r$ has order 4), so $sr\neq rs$. Contradiction. ∎

↑ Problem

18. Let $G=\{2^m3^n: m,n\in\mathbb{Z}\}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^*$. It is a subgroup: $1=2^03^0\in G$, $(2^m3^n)(2^{m'}3^{n'})=2^{m+m'}3^{n+n'}$, and $(2^m3^n)^{-1}=2^{-m}3^{-n}$.

Let $H=\langle2\rangle=\{2^m\}$ and $K=\langle3\rangle=\{3^n\}$. Then $G=HK$ by definition, and $hk=kh$ since $\mathbb{Q}^*$ is abelian. If $2^m=3^n$: when $m>0$, also $n>0$ (as $3^n=2^m>1$) and an even integer equals an odd one, impossible; when $m<0$, then $n<0$ and $2^{-m}=3^{-n}$ is the same contradiction; so $m=0$, and then $3^n=1$ forces $n=0$. So $H\cap K=\{1\}$ and $G$ is the internal direct product of $H$ and $K$.

Define $\psi:\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}\to G$ by $\psi(m,n)=2^m3^n$. It is a homomorphism: $$\begin{aligned}\psi((m,n)+(m',n')) &= 2^{m+m'}3^{n+n'}\\ &= \psi(m,n)\psi(m',n')\end{aligned}.$$ It is onto by definition of $G$. It is one-to-one: if $2^m3^n=1$, then $2^m=3^{-n}\in H\cap K=\{1\}$, so $m=n=0$. Hence $G\cong\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}$. ∎

↑ Problem

19. Facts about $D_k$ used (Judson, Ch. 5): $D_k$ is generated by $r$ of order $k$ and $s$ of order 2 with $srs=r^{-1}$; its $2k$ elements $r^is^j$ ($0\leq i<k$, $j\in\{0,1\}$) are distinct; and $sr^i=r^{-i}s$, so

$$(r^is^j)(r^ls^m)=r^{\,i+(-1)^jl}\,s^{\,j+m}.\tag{*}$$

Lemma. If a group contains $a$ of order $k$ and $b$ of order 2 with $bab=a^{-1}$, $b\notin\langle a\rangle$, then $L=\{a^ib^j: 0\leq i<k, j\in\{0,1\}\}$ is a subgroup of order $2k$ isomorphic to $D_k$. Indeed, $ba^i=a^{-i}b$, so products in $L$ follow rule (*) and $L$ is closed; it has $2k$ distinct elements (if $a^i=a^lb$ then $b\in\langle a\rangle$); and $r^is^j\mapsto a^ib^j$ is a bijection $D_k\to L$ that preserves (*), hence an isomorphism.

$S_3\times\mathbb{Z}_2\cong D_6$. In $D_6$ ($r$ of order 6), let $H=\{r^{2i}s^j\}$ and $K=\{e,r^3\}$. Here $r^2$ has order 3, $s(r^2)s=r^{-2}$, and $s\notin\langle r^2\rangle$, so by the Lemma $H$ is a subgroup of order 6 isomorphic to $D_3$. And $D_3\cong S_3$: in $S_3$, $a=(1\,2\,3)$ has order 3, $b=(1\,2)$ has order 2, $bab=(1\,3\,2)=a^{-1}$, so by the Lemma $S_3$ (which has order 6) is $\cong D_3$.

$r^3$ commutes with every element: it commutes with $r$, and $sr^3=r^{-3}s=r^3s$. So $hk=kh$ for $h\in H$, $k\in K$. $H\cap K=\{e\}$ since $r^3\neq r^{2i}$ ($3$ is odd, $2i$ is even, mod 6) and $r^3$ is not a reflection. Then $|HK|=6\cdot2=12$, so $HK=D_6$. Thus $D_6$ is the internal direct product of $H$ and $K$, so $$\begin{aligned}D_6 &\cong H\times K\\ &\cong S_3\times\mathbb{Z}_2\end{aligned}$$ (using 9.47 for the last step).

Conjecture: $D_{2n}\cong D_n\times\mathbb{Z}_2$ whenever $n\geq3$ is odd. (For $n=3$ this is the statement above, since $D_3\cong S_3$.)

Proof. In $D_{2n}$ ($r$ of order $2n$), let $H=\{r^{2i}s^j: 0\leq i<n, j\in\{0,1\}\}$ and $K=\{e,r^n\}$. $r^2$ has order $n$, $sr^2s=r^{-2}$ and $s\notin\langle r^2\rangle$, so by the Lemma $H$ is a subgroup isomorphic to $D_n$. $r^n$ is central: $sr^n=r^{-n}s=r^ns$ (since $r^{2n}=e$). $H\cap K=\{e\}$: $r^n$ is not a reflection, and $r^n=r^{2i}$ would need $2n\mid n-2i$, impossible because $n-2i$ is odd. So $|HK|=2n\cdot2=4n=|D_{2n}|$, $D_{2n}$ is the internal direct product of $H$ and $K$, and $$\begin{aligned}D_{2n} &\cong H\times K\\ &\cong D_n\times\mathbb{Z}_2\end{aligned}.$$

(For $n$ even the conjecture fails, e.g. $n=4$: the center of $D_8$ is $\{e,r^4\}$ of order 2, while $D_4\times\mathbb{Z}_2$ has center $Z(D_4)\times\mathbb{Z}_2$ of order 4.) ∎

22. $H\cap K$ is a subgroup of both $H$ and $K$, so by Lagrange its order divides $\gcd(4,5)=1$: $H\cap K=\{e\}$. Then the 20 products $hk$ are distinct: if $hk=h'k'$ then $h'^{-1}h=k'k^{-1}\in H\cap K=\{e\}$, so $h=h'$ and $k=k'$. Hence $|HK|=4\cdot5=20=|G|$ and $G=HK$. Together with $hk=kh$ (given), all three conditions hold, so $G$ is the internal direct product of $H$ and $K$. ∎

47. Let $\phi:G\to\overline{G}$ and $\psi:H\to\overline{H}$ be isomorphisms. Define $\Theta:G\times H\to\overline{G}\times\overline{H}$ by $\Theta(g,h)=(\phi(g),\psi(h))$.

Homomorphism: $$\begin{aligned}\Theta((g,h)(g',h')) &= \Theta(gg',hh')\\ &= (\phi(g)\phi(g'),\psi(h)\psi(h'))\\ &= (\phi(g),\psi(h))(\phi(g'),\psi(h'))\\ &= \Theta(g,h)\Theta(g',h')\end{aligned}.$$

Bijection: $\Lambda(x,y)=(\phi^{-1}(x),\psi^{-1}(y))$ satisfies $\Lambda\circ\Theta=\mathrm{id}$ and $\Theta\circ\Lambda=\mathrm{id}$. So $\Theta$ is an isomorphism and $G\times H\cong\overline{G}\times\overline{H}$. ∎

6. A homomorphism $\phi:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}_{12}$ is determined by $a=\phi(1)$: $\phi(n)=\phi(1+\cdots+1)=na$ for $n>0$, $\phi(0)=0$, and $\phi(-n)=-\phi(n)$. Conversely, for each $a\in\mathbb{Z}_{12}$ the map $\phi_a(n)=na\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 12)$ is a homomorphism, since $\phi_a(m+n)=(m+n)a=ma+na$.

So there are exactly 12 homomorphisms, $\phi_a(n)=an\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12$ for $a=0,1,\ldots,11$. For each, with $d=\gcd(a,12)$: the image is $\langle a\rangle=\langle d\rangle$, which has order $12/d$; the kernel is $$\begin{aligned}\{n: 12\mid an\} &= \{n: \frac{12}{d}\mid n\}\\ &= \frac{12}{d}\mathbb{Z}\end{aligned}.$$ For example, $\phi_0$ is trivial, and $\phi_1,\phi_5,\phi_7,\phi_{11}$ are onto with kernel $12\mathbb{Z}$. ∎

14. Let $L=\phi^{-1}(H)=\{g\in G: gN\in H\}$. Subgroup: $\phi(e)=N$ is the identity of $G/N$ and lies in $H$, so $e\in L$. If $g_1,g_2\in L$, then $\phi(g_1g_2^{-1})=\phi(g_1)\phi(g_2)^{-1}\in H$ because $H$ is a subgroup, so $g_1g_2^{-1}\in L$. Hence $L$ is a subgroup of $G$.

Order: write $H=\{g_1N,\ldots,g_tN\}$ with $t=|H|$ distinct cosets. Then $g\in L$ iff $gN=g_iN$ for some $i$, iff $g\in g_iN$ for some $i$. So $L=g_1N\cup\cdots\cup g_tN$. Distinct cosets are disjoint and each has $|N|$ elements, so $|L|=t\,|N|=|H|\cdot|N|$. ∎

16. Define $\psi:G\to G/H\times G/K$ by $\psi(g)=(gH,gK)$. (Both quotients are groups since $H,K$ are normal.) It is a homomorphism: $$\begin{aligned}\psi(gg') &= (gg'H,gg'K)\\ &= (gH\,g'H,\;gK\,g'K)\\ &= \psi(g)\psi(g')\end{aligned}.$$

Kernel: $\psi(g)=(H,K)$ iff $gH=H$ and $gK=K$ iff $g\in H$ and $g\in K$. So $\ker\psi=H\cap K=\{e\}$ and $\psi$ is one-to-one. The image $\psi(G)$ of a homomorphism is a subgroup of $G/H\times G/K$, and $\psi:G\to\psi(G)$ is a bijective homomorphism. So $G\cong\psi(G)$, a subgroup of $G/H\times G/K$. ∎

↑ Problem

(a) We use the subgroup test: a nonempty subset $S\subseteq G$ is a subgroup if $xy^{-1}\in S$ whenever $x,y\in S$. $HK$ is nonempty since $e=ee\in HK$. Let $x=hk$ and $y=h'k'$ be in $HK$. Since $G$ is abelian,

$$xy^{-1}=hk\,(h'k')^{-1}=hk\,k'^{-1}h'^{-1}=(hh'^{-1})(kk'^{-1}).$$

Here $hh'^{-1}\in H$ and $kk'^{-1}\in K$ because $H$ and $K$ are subgroups. So $xy^{-1}\in HK$, and $HK$ is a subgroup of $G$. ∎

(b) Take $G=S_3$, $H=\{(1),(1\,2)\}$ and $K=\{(1),(1\,3)\}$, two distinct subgroups of order 2. Then

$$HK=\{(1)(1),\;(1)(1\,3),\;(1\,2)(1),\;(1\,2)(1\,3)\}=\{(1),\,(1\,3),\,(1\,2),\,(1\,3\,2)\},$$

where $(1\,2)(1\,3)$, read right to left, sends $1\to3\to3$, $3\to1\to2$, $2\to2\to1$, so it is $(1\,3\,2)$. These are 4 distinct elements, and $4$ does not divide $|S_3|=6$, so by Lagrange's Theorem $HK$ is not a subgroup. (Directly: $(1\,3),(1\,2)\in HK$ but $(1\,3)(1\,2)=(1\,2\,3)\notin HK$, so $HK$ is not closed.) ∎
