Problems

1. For $p_2=ax^2+bx+c,\ p_1=dx+e,\ p_0=f$, let $D=p_2\frac{d^2}{dx^2}+p_1\frac{d}{dx}+p_0$.

(a) Why does $D$ preserve $\mathcal{P}_m(\mathbb{R})$? (b) Find all diagonal entries of $D$ in the basis $(1,x,\dots,x^m)$.

(c) If $0<a<d$, prove $D$ is diagonalizable on $\mathcal{P}_m(\mathbb{R})$. (d) For $D=(x^2-x)\frac{d^2}{dx^2}+(2x-1)\frac{d}{dx}$, find a basis of eigenvectors on $\mathcal{P}_3(\mathbb{R})$.

Solutions

↑ Problem

Solution 1. (a),(b) $D$ is upper-triangular in $(1,x,\dots,x^m)$.

Compute on a monomial: $(x^k)''=k(k-1)x^{k-2}$ and $(x^k)'=kx^{k-1}$, so $D(x^k)=[a\,k(k-1)+dk+f]\,x^k+[b\,k(k-1)+ek]\,x^{k-1}+[c\,k(k-1)]\,x^{k-2}.$

(a) Each $D(x^k)$ has degree $\leq k\leq m$, so $D$ maps $\mathcal{P}_m(\mathbb{R})$ into itself (and is linear).

(b) $D(x^k)$ only involves $x^{k},x^{k-1},x^{k-2}$, so the matrix is upper-triangular; the diagonal entry for $x^k$ is the coefficient of $x^k$: $a\,k(k-1)+dk+f$, for $k=0,1,\dots,m$.

(c) Being triangular, the eigenvalues of $D$ are the diagonal entries $\lambda_k=a\,k(k-1)+dk+f$. Their gaps are $\lambda_{k+1}-\lambda_k=2ak+d>0$ (as $a,d>0$), so $\lambda_0<\lambda_1<\cdots<\lambda_m$ are distinct. An operator on the $(m{+}1)$-dimensional $\mathcal{P}_m(\mathbb{R})$ with $m{+}1$ distinct eigenvalues is diagonalizable.

(d) Here $a=1,b=-1,c=0,d=2,e=-1,f=0$, so $\lambda_k=k(k-1)+2k=k(k+1)$: eigenvalues $0,2,6,12$. Solving $D(p)=\lambda_k p$ degree by degree gives the eigenvectors (verified by substitution): $\lambda=0:\ 1;\quad \lambda=2:\ x-\frac12;\quad \lambda=6:\ x^2-x+\frac16;\quad \lambda=12:\ x^3-\frac32 x^2+\frac35 x-\frac{1}{20}.$ ∎

↑ Problem

Solution 2. (a) Gram-Schmidt (orthogonal, not normalized).

$e_0=1$; $e_1=x-\frac12$; $e_2=x^2-x+\frac16$; $e_3=x^3-\frac32 x^2+\frac35 x-\frac{1}{20}$, with $\langle e_0,e_0\rangle=1,\ \langle e_1,e_1\rangle=\frac{1}{12},\ \langle e_2,e_2\rangle=\frac{1}{180},\ \langle e_3,e_3\rangle=\frac{1}{2800}$. (These are the shifted Legendre polynomials on $[0,1]$, and they equal the eigenvectors from 1(d).)

(b) $p_U(f)=\sum_k c_k e_k$ with $c_k=\langle f,e_k\rangle/\langle e_k,e_k\rangle$ and $\langle\sqrt{x},x^k\rangle=\int_0^1 x^{k+1/2}dx=2/(2k+3)$. The $c_k$ are $\frac{2}{3},\ \frac{4}{5},\ -\frac{4}{7},\ \frac{8}{9}$, giving $p_U(\sqrt{x})=\frac{8}{9}x^3-\frac{40}{21}x^2+\frac{40}{21}x+\frac{8}{63}.$

(c) Values (to $3$ decimals):

        x        sqrt(x)    p_U(f)(x)
        0.00      0.000      0.127
        0.25      0.500      0.498
        0.50      0.707      0.714
        0.75      0.866      0.859
        1.00      1.000      1.016

(d) The eigenvectors of $D$ from 1(d) are exactly the Gram-Schmidt orthogonal polynomials from 2(a). Reason: $D$ is self-adjoint for $\langle\,,\rangle$ (a Sturm-Liouville operator), so its eigenvectors are orthogonal; since the degree-$k$ eigenvector has degree exactly $k$, it must be the $k$-th orthogonal polynomial. This pattern continues for every $\mathcal{P}_m$: the Gram-Schmidt basis is always a basis of eigenvectors of $D$. ∎

↑ Problem

Solution 3. Polarization (real case).

$\|u+v\|^2=\|u\|^2+2\langle u,v\rangle+\|v\|^2$ and $\|u-v\|^2=\|u\|^2-2\langle u,v\rangle+\|v\|^2$ (using $\langle u,v\rangle=\langle v,u\rangle$). Subtracting: $\|u+v\|^2-\|u-v\|^2=4\langle u,v\rangle$, so $\langle u,v\rangle=\frac{\|u+v\|^2-\|u-v\|^2}{4}$. ∎

↑ Problem

Solution 4. Exactly $2^m$ such lists.

Run Gram-Schmidt: for each $j$, $e_j$ must lie in $\mathrm{span}(v_1,\dots,v_j)$ and be orthogonal to $\mathrm{span}(v_1,\dots,v_{j-1})$. That orthogonal complement inside $\mathrm{span}(v_1,\dots,v_j)$ is $1$-dimensional (the dimension goes up by one), and a unit vector in a $1$-dimensional real space is determined up to sign - exactly $2$ choices for each $e_j$. The choices for $j=1,\dots,m$ are independent, and every such choice satisfies all the span conditions. So there are exactly $2\cdot 2\cdots 2=2^m$ orthonormal lists. ∎

↑ Problem

Solution 5. $\dim U^{\perp}=\dim V-\dim U$.

Every $v\in V$ splits as $v=P_U v+(v-P_U v)$ with $P_U v\in U$ and $v-P_U v\in U^{\perp}$, so $V=U+U^{\perp}$. If $w\in U\cap U^{\perp}$ then $\langle w,w\rangle=0$, so $w=0$; hence $V=U\oplus U^{\perp}$. Taking dimensions, $\dim V=\dim U+\dim U^{\perp}$, i.e. $\dim U^{\perp}=\dim V-\dim U$. ∎
