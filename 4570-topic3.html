1(a) (Claude, not official)

Linear.

Let $x = (a, b)$, $y = (c, d) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ and $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$.

Additivity: $T(x + y) = T(a + c, b + d) = (2(a + c) - (b + d),\ (a + c) + 3(b + d)) = (2a - b, a + 3b) + (2c - d, c + 3d) = T(x) + T(y)$.

Homogeneity: $T(\alpha x) = T(\alpha a, \alpha b) = (2\alpha a - \alpha b,\ \alpha a + 3\alpha b) = \alpha(2a - b, a + 3b) = \alpha T(x)$.

(Equivalently, $T = L_A$ with $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$, and every $L_A$ is linear.)

1(b) (Claude, not official)

Not linear. Homogeneity (and additivity) fails because of the $b^2$.

Take $x = (0, 1)$ and $\alpha = 2$. Then $T(2x) = T(0, 2) = (-2, 4)$, but $2T(x) = 2(-1, 1) = (-2, 2)$. Since $(-2, 4) \neq (-2, 2)$, $T(\alpha x) \neq \alpha T(x)$.

Additivity fails too: $T((0,1) + (0,1)) = (-2, 4)$ while $T(0,1) + T(0,1) = (-2, 2)$.

Let $z = (z_1, z_2)$, $w = (w_1, w_2) \in \mathbb{C}^2$ and $\alpha \in \mathbb{C}$.

1(h) (Claude, not official)

Not linear over $\mathbb{C}$. Additivity holds ($\overline{z + w} = \overline{z} + \overline{w}$), but homogeneity with complex scalars fails.

Take $\alpha = i$ and $z = 1$: $T(i \cdot 1) = \overline{i} = -i$, but $i \cdot T(1) = i \cdot \overline{1} = i$. Since $-i \neq i$, $T(\alpha z) \neq \alpha T(z)$.

In general $T(\alpha z) = \overline{\alpha}\,\overline{z}$, which equals $\alpha \overline{z}$ only when $\alpha$ is real or $z = 0$.

3(a) (Claude, not official)

Compute $\mathcal{N}(T_k)$: we need $a + kb = 0$ and $ka + b = 0$. The first gives $a = -kb$; substituting into the second, $-k^2 b + b = (1 - k^2) b = 0$.

If $k^2 \neq 1$, then $b = 0$, hence $a = 0$, so $\mathcal{N}(T_k) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$.

If $k = 1$, the equations are both $a + b = 0$, and $(1, -1) \in \mathcal{N}(T_1)$. If $k = -1$, both equations say $a - b = 0$, and $(1, 1) \in \mathcal{N}(T_{-1})$.

Since a linear map is one-to-one iff its nullspace is $\{\mathbf{0}\}$: $T_k$ is one-to-one exactly when $k \neq 1$ and $k \neq -1$. (Equivalently, $\det\begin{pmatrix} 1 & k \\ k & 1 \end{pmatrix} = 1 - k^2 \neq 0$.)

4(a) (Claude, not official)

Linear. If $A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$, $A' = \begin{pmatrix} a' & b' \\ c' & d' \end{pmatrix}$ and $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}$, then $\alpha A + \beta A'$ has entries $\alpha a + \beta a'$, etc., and

$T(\alpha A + \beta A') = (\alpha(a + d) + \beta(a' + d'),\ \alpha(b - c) + \beta(b' - c')) = \alpha T(A) + \beta T(A')$.

Nullspace. $a + d = 0$ and $b - c = 0$, so $d = -a$, $c = b$:

$$\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ b & -a \end{pmatrix} = a\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} + b\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

These two matrices are linearly independent (different positions), so a basis for $\mathcal{N}(T)$ is $\left\{\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\right\}$ and $\mathrm{nullity}(T) = 2$.

Range. $T\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = (1, 0)$ and $T\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = (0, 1)$, so $\mathcal{R}(T) = \mathbb{R}^2$ with basis $\{(1, 0), (0, 1)\}$; $\mathrm{rank}(T) = 2$. (Check: $2 + 2 = 4 = \dim M_{2,2}(\mathbb{R})$.)

Not one-to-one (nullity $2 \neq 0$); onto.

4(b) (Claude, not official)

Linear. Transpose is linear: $(\alpha A + \beta B)^{\mathsf T} = \alpha A^{\mathsf T} + \beta B^{\mathsf T}$. So $T(\alpha A + \beta B) = \alpha A + \beta B + \alpha A^{\mathsf T} + \beta B^{\mathsf T} = \alpha T(A) + \beta T(B)$.

For $A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$: $T(A) = \begin{pmatrix} 2a & b + c \\ b + c & 2d \end{pmatrix}$.

Nullspace. $2a = 0$, $b + c = 0$, $2d = 0$, so $A = b\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ (the skew-symmetric matrices). Basis $\left\{\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\right\}$, $\mathrm{nullity}(T) = 1$.

Range. Every $T(A)$ is symmetric, since $(A + A^{\mathsf T})^{\mathsf T} = A^{\mathsf T} + A$. Conversely, if $S$ is symmetric then $T(\tfrac12 S) = \tfrac12 S + \tfrac12 S^{\mathsf T} = S$. So $\mathcal{R}(T)$ is the space of symmetric $2 \times 2$ matrices, with basis

$$\left\{\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\right\},$$

and $\mathrm{rank}(T) = 3$ (check: $3 + 1 = 4$).

Not one-to-one (nullity 1); not onto (rank $3 < 4$; e.g. $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ is not symmetric, so not in the range).
