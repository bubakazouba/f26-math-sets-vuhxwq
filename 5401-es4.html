Chapter 5

↑ Problem

3. A $k$-cycle is a product of $k-1$ transpositions: $(a_1\,a_2\,\ldots\,a_k)=(a_1\,a_k)(a_1\,a_{k-1})\cdots(a_1\,a_2)$ (right to left).

(a) $(1\,4\,3\,5\,6)=(1\,6)(1\,5)(1\,3)(1\,4)$: four transpositions, so EVEN.

(b) $(1\,5\,6)(2\,3\,4)=(1\,6)(1\,5)(2\,4)(2\,3)$: four transpositions, so EVEN.

(c) First multiply out: $1\to4\to2$, $2\to1\to4$, $4\to2\to6$, $6\to6\to1$, $3$ fixed. So $(1\,4\,2\,6)(1\,4\,2)=(1\,2\,4\,6)=(1\,6)(1\,4)(1\,2)$: three transpositions, so ODD. (Check: $(1\,4\,2\,6)$ is odd and $(1\,4\,2)$ is even, and odd times even is odd.) ∎

↑ Problem

13. Let $\sigma_i$ have length $\ell_i$, so $\sigma_i$ has order $\ell_i$. Disjoint cycles commute, so $\sigma^k=\sigma_1^k\sigma_2^k\cdots\sigma_m^k$ for every $k$. The factors $\sigma_i^k$ move pairwise disjoint sets of points, so $\sigma^k$ is the identity iff each $\sigma_i^k$ is the identity, i.e. iff $\ell_i\mid k$ for every $i$. The least positive such $k$ is $\mathrm{lcm}(\ell_1,\ldots,\ell_m)$, which is therefore the order of $\sigma$. ∎

↑ Problem

36. Write $\alpha$ as a product of $a$ transpositions and $\beta$ as a product of $b$ transpositions. Reversing the order gives $\alpha^{-1}$ as a product of the same $a$ transpositions (each transposition is its own inverse), and likewise $\beta^{-1}$ uses $b$. So $\alpha^{-1}\beta^{-1}\alpha\beta$ is a product of $a+b+a+b=2(a+b)$ transpositions, an even number. Hence it is even. ∎

1. The order of an element divides the order of the group (a corollary of Lagrange's Theorem: $|\langle g\rangle|$ divides $|G|$). So $5\mid |G|$ and $7\mid |G|$. Since $\gcd(5,7)=1$, $35\mid |G|$, and a positive multiple of 35 is at least 35. ∎

↑ Problem

5. Each group here is abelian, so left and right cosets coincide.

(a) $H=\langle 8\rangle=\{0,8,16\}$ has index $24/3=8$. The cosets are $k+H$ for $k=0,\ldots,7$: $\{0,8,16\}$, $\{1,9,17\}$, $\{2,10,18\}$, $\{3,11,19\}$, $\{4,12,20\}$, $\{5,13,21\}$, $\{6,14,22\}$, $\{7,15,23\}$.

(b) $U(8)=\{1,3,5,7\}$ and $H=\langle 3\rangle=\{1,3\}$ (since $3^2=9\equiv1$). The cosets are $1H=3H=\{1,3\}$ and $5H=7H=\{5,7\}$ (since $5\cdot3=15\equiv7$).

(c) The cosets of $3\mathbb{Z}$ in $\mathbb{Z}$ are $3\mathbb{Z}=\{\ldots,-3,0,3,\ldots\}$, $1+3\mathbb{Z}=\{\ldots,-2,1,4,\ldots\}$, and $2+3\mathbb{Z}=\{\ldots,-1,2,5,\ldots\}$. ∎

↑ Problem

6. For $g,A\in GL_2(\mathbb{R})$: $A\in g\,SL_2(\mathbb{R})$ iff $g^{-1}A\in SL_2(\mathbb{R})$ iff $\det(g^{-1}A)=1$ iff $\det A=\det g$. So the left coset $g\,SL_2(\mathbb{R})$ is exactly the set of invertible matrices whose determinant equals $\det g$. Every nonzero real $t$ occurs as a determinant (e.g. $\mathrm{diag}(t,1)$), so the cosets are in bijection with $\mathbb{R}^*=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$, one coset per value of the determinant. Hence the index $[GL_2(\mathbb{R}):SL_2(\mathbb{R})]$ is infinite (it has the cardinality of $\mathbb{R}^*$, uncountable). ∎

↑ Problem

8. Suppose $x^2\equiv-1$ (mod $p$). Then $p\nmid x$ (otherwise $0\equiv-1$, i.e. $p\mid1$). Since $p-1=4n+2=2(2n+1)$, we get $x^{p-1}=(x^2)^{2n+1}\equiv(-1)^{2n+1}=-1$ (mod $p$). But Fermat's Little Theorem gives $x^{p-1}\equiv1$ (mod $p$). So $1\equiv-1$, i.e. $p\mid2$, impossible because $p\geq3$. So there is no solution. ∎

↑ Problem

9. Two cosets $q+\mathbb{Z}$ and $q'+\mathbb{Z}$ are equal iff $q-q'\in\mathbb{Z}$. For distinct positive integers $j<k$ we have $0<\frac{1}{j}-\frac{1}{k}<1$, so $\frac{1}{j}-\frac{1}{k}\notin\mathbb{Z}$. Hence the cosets $\frac{1}{k}+\mathbb{Z}$, $k=1,2,3,\ldots$, are pairwise distinct. There are infinitely many cosets, so $[\mathbb{Q}:\mathbb{Z}]$ is infinite. ∎

11. (a)$\Rightarrow$(c): trivial.

(c)$\Rightarrow$(d): $g_1=g_1e\in g_1H\subset g_2H$, so $g_1=g_2h$ for some $h\in H$. Then $g_2=g_1h^{-1}\in g_1H$.

(d)$\Rightarrow$(e): if $g_2=g_1h$ with $h\in H$, then $g_1^{-1}g_2=h\in H$.

14. Let $m$ be the order of $g$ (finite, since $g^n=e$; the case $n=0$ is trivial as $m\mid0$). Divide: $n=qm+r$ with $0\leq r<m$. Then $g^r=g^n(g^m)^{-q}=e\cdot e=e$. Since $m$ is the least positive exponent giving $e$ and $0\leq r<m$, we must have $r=0$. So $m\mid n$. ∎

Additional problem: subgroups of $S_4$

↑ Problem

$S_4$ has order 24, so by Lagrange a subgroup has order 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12 or 24. There are 30 subgroups in total:

$\bullet$ Order 1 (1 subgroup): $\{e\}$.

$\bullet$ Order 2 (9 subgroups): the six $\langle(i\,j)\rangle$, namely $\langle(1\,2)\rangle,\langle(1\,3)\rangle,\langle(1\,4)\rangle,\langle(2\,3)\rangle,\langle(2\,4)\rangle,\langle(3\,4)\rangle$. Also the three $\langle(1\,2)(3\,4)\rangle,\ \langle(1\,3)(2\,4)\rangle,\ \langle(1\,4)(2\,3)\rangle$.

$\bullet$ Order 3 (4 subgroups): $\langle(1\,2\,3)\rangle,\ \langle(1\,2\,4)\rangle,\ \langle(1\,3\,4)\rangle,\ \langle(2\,3\,4)\rangle$, one for each 3-element subset of $\{1,2,3,4\}$.

$\bullet$ Order 4 (7 subgroups). Cyclic: $\langle(1\,2\,3\,4)\rangle,\ \langle(1\,2\,4\,3)\rangle,\ \langle(1\,3\,2\,4)\rangle$. The normal Klein four-group $V=\{e,(1\,2)(3\,4),(1\,3)(2\,4),(1\,4)(2\,3)\}$. Three more Klein four-groups: $\{e,(1\,2),(3\,4),(1\,2)(3\,4)\}$, $\{e,(1\,3),(2\,4),(1\,3)(2\,4)\}$, $\{e,(1\,4),(2\,3),(1\,4)(2\,3)\}$.

$\bullet$ Order 6 (4 subgroups): for each $i$, the stabilizer of $i$ (all permutations of the other three points, $\cong S_3$). For example, the stabilizer of 4 is $\{e,(1\,2),(1\,3),(2\,3),(1\,2\,3),(1\,3\,2)\}$.

$\bullet$ Order 8 (3 subgroups, each $\cong D_4$, the symmetries of a square with vertices labeled in cyclic order): $\{e,(1\,2\,3\,4),(1\,3)(2\,4),(1\,4\,3\,2),(1\,3),(2\,4),(1\,2)(3\,4),(1\,4)(2\,3)\}$ $\{e,(1\,2\,4\,3),(1\,4)(2\,3),(1\,3\,4\,2),(1\,4),(2\,3),(1\,2)(3\,4),(1\,3)(2\,4)\}$ $\{e,(1\,3\,2\,4),(1\,2)(3\,4),(1\,4\,2\,3),(1\,2),(3\,4),(1\,3)(2\,4),(1\,4)(2\,3)\}$

$\bullet$ Order 12 (1 subgroup): $A_4$.$\bullet$ Order 24 (1 subgroup): $S_4$.Total: $1+9+4+7+4+3+1+1=30$. Which are subgroups of $A_4$? Exactly those made entirely of even permutations. Transpositions and 4-cycles are odd, while 3-cycles and products of two disjoint transpositions are even. That leaves 10: $\{e\}$; the three $\langle(i\,j)(k\,l)\rangle$; the four $\langle(a\,b\,c)\rangle$; $V$; and $A_4$ itself. None of order 6: each $S_3$ above contains transpositions, and in fact $A_4$ has no subgroup of order 6 at all. None of order 8 either, since $8\nmid12$. ∎

Challenge problems

↑ Problem

6.22. $\phi(n)$ counts $1\leq x\leq n$ with $\gcd(x,n)=1$, i.e. $x$ divisible by none of $p_1,\ldots,p_k$. Let $A_i=\{1\leq x\leq n: p_i\mid x\}$. For distinct $i_1,\ldots,i_r$, the set $A_{i_1}\cap\cdots\cap A_{i_r}$ consists of the multiples of $p_{i_1}\cdots p_{i_r}$ up to $n$. It has exactly $n/(p_{i_1}\cdots p_{i_r})$ elements, since that product divides $n$. By inclusion-exclusion, $\phi(n)=n-\sum_i\frac{n}{p_i}+\sum_{i<j}\frac{n}{p_ip_j}-\cdots+(-1)^k\frac{n}{p_1\cdots p_k}=n\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(1-\frac{1}{p_i}\right)$,

where the last step is expanding the product. ∎

↑ Problem

6.23. Sort the integers $1\leq x\leq n$ by $d=\gcd(x,n)$, a divisor of $n$. The $x$ with $\gcd(x,n)=d$ are $x=dy$ with $1\leq y\leq n/d$ and $\gcd(y,n/d)=1$, and there are exactly $\phi(n/d)$ of them. Every $x$ lands in exactly one class, so $n=\sum_{d\mid n}\phi(n/d)$. As $d$ runs over the divisors of $n$, so does $n/d$; hence $n=\sum_{d\mid n}\phi(d)$. ∎
